# The Warning: Rudolf Vrba and Alfred Wetzler, 1944

*Working title. Animated documentary, about 10 minutes, English narration.*
**Status:** DRAFT v1, for review (step 2). About 1,460 narrated words.

## How to read this

- Plain paragraphs are narration. Everything else (headings, `> Picture` notes, `[F#]` tags, cards) is not read aloud.
- `[F#]` points to the claim and its sources in `FACTS.md`.
- `> Picture` notes are a first idea of the visuals for each passage. They will change once the voice timing is set.
- Timings assume about 145 words per minute plus pauses. The scenes will be recut to the real narration.

## Delivery

- Calm, low and unhurried. No dramatic emphasis; the facts carry the weight.
- Leave about 1 s between paragraphs and about 2 s at each part break.
- Numbers are written the way they should be spoken.

## Opening card (no narration, about 6 s)

*Animated reconstruction, based on the Vrba–Wetzler report of April 1944, Rudolf Vrba's memoir and historical research. People are shown as figures, not portraits.*

## Cold open (0:00–0:25)

> Picture: black screen with charcoal grain. A single line of railway track is drawn on twos and runs into darkness. Slow push toward a stack of boards in silhouette against a grey night sky. Faint camp lights, far off.

April 1944. Two young men lie hidden inside a stack of timber at Auschwitz-Birkenau. Around them, the camp is searching. [F1]

They want to live. And they want to warn the world before the next trains arrive. [F2]

## 1 · Slovakia, 1942 (0:25–1:40)

> Picture: a pencil map of Central Europe on off-white paper, with slightly wobbly borders. Slovakia is washed in sepia. A small town dot is labelled Topoľčany.

He was born Walter Rosenberg, in 1924, in the small Slovak town of Topoľčany. [F3]

In 1939, Slovakia broke away from Czechoslovakia and became a separate state, under a nationalist regime allied with Nazi Germany. [F4] Laws against Jews followed, one after another. At fifteen, Walter was expelled from his school in Bratislava. He worked as a labourer and kept studying on his own. [F5]

> Picture: printed-paper texture. A government gazette page (a period document if licensed, otherwise drawn) with paragraphs struck through in ink, one by one.

In September 1941 came the so-called Jewish Code: nearly three hundred paragraphs, modelled on Germany's Nuremberg Laws, that pushed Slovakia's Jews out of public life. [F6]

In March 1942, the deportations began. The Slovak state organised the trains and paid Germany five hundred Reichsmarks for every Jew it sent away. [F7] By October, about fifty-eight thousand people were gone. [F8]

> Picture: a faceless charcoal figure of a young man at a window at night. Then a road toward a border marker, drawn in pencil and erased as he walks.

Walter was seventeen. He decided not to wait. He would make his way to England and join the Czechoslovak army in exile. [F9]

He tried to cross into Hungary. It ended in arrest. He was held at the Nováky transit camp, slipped out, and was caught again. [F10]

## 2 · Majdanek, then Auschwitz (1:40–2:15)

> Picture: a freight wagon in silhouette, small against a huge empty paper sky. The map returns, and an ink line crawls north-east to Lublin, then west to Oświęcim.

On the fourteenth of June 1942, he was deported to Majdanek, a camp on the edge of Lublin, in occupied Poland. [F11]

Two weeks later he was moved again. On the thirtieth of June he arrived at Auschwitz, and became prisoner number forty-four thousand and seventy. [F12]

> Picture: the number 44070 written in ink on paper, never on skin.

A few kilometres from the main camp lay Birkenau, the largest killing centre the SS ever built. [F13] Most people who arrived on the Jewish transports were never registered at all. They were killed within hours. [F14]

---

## 3 · Kanada and the ramp (2:15–3:40)

> Picture: a warehouse interior in charcoal and sepia wash. Mountains of suitcases as soft overlapping shapes. Possibly one accent-colour detail; to be decided, as it may be too much.

In August 1942, he was put to work in a detail the prisoners called Kanada. They named it after a country they imagined as impossibly rich. [F15]

Kanada sorted what the deported had brought with them, believing they were being resettled: suitcases, coats, shoes, spectacles, photographs, money sewn into hems. Most of it was shipped to Germany. [F16]

> Picture: the ramp at night in a long horizontal composition. The train is a dark band, floodlights are smudged white, and silhouettes step down. Bags are left on the platform. The figures divide into two groups and leave the frame. Nothing else is shown.

He was also sent to the ramp, the railway platform where the transports arrived. The wagon doors opened. People were made to leave their luggage and were divided in minutes: a few were sent into the camp to work; the rest, the old, the sick, mothers and children, were sent to the gas chambers. [F17]

In the summer of 1943, he became a registrar, a camp clerk, in the quarantine section of Birkenau. [F18] It gave him what few prisoners had: some freedom to move, and a view of the camp's machinery. He could see the trains arrive and the trucks drive on. [F19]

> Picture: close on the faceless figure in a doorway. Pencil tally marks appear on the paper texture and are rubbed out, over and over: memory, not records.

So he started to count. Paper could get him killed, so he kept nothing in writing. He memorised where each transport came from, roughly how many people it carried, and how few were let into the camp. [F20]

## 4 · Hungary (3:40–4:40)

> Picture: the map again, with Hungary washed in sepia. A dotted line of German troops enters, drawn like a stain spreading through the paper fibres.

By the spring of 1944, the largest Jewish community still left in Europe was in Hungary: roughly three quarters of a million people. Hungary was Germany's ally, and until then its government had resisted German demands to deport them. [F21]

On the nineteenth of March 1944, German troops occupied the country. [F22]

> Picture: Birkenau's gatehouse in silhouette. Rails are drawn stroke by stroke through the arch and into the camp.

In Birkenau, the preparations could already be seen. Prisoners were extending the railway line through the gate, so that trains could run straight into the camp, almost to the crematoria. [F23] Vrba later recalled SS men joking about the "Hungarian salami" that was on its way. [F24]

> Picture: rows of barracks under a low sky. Windows go dark one at a time. No people are shown.

He already knew what a warning was worth inside the wire. That March he had tried to warn the Jewish families from Theresienstadt in the neighbouring camp. On the night of the eighth, nearly three thousand eight hundred of them were murdered. [F25]

A warning had to reach people before they boarded the trains. Someone had to get out. [F26]

## 5 · The woodpile (4:40–6:30)

*This is the test scene for step 4.*

> Picture: a bird's-eye pencil diagram of two rings, inner and outer, drawn with a wobbly compass line. The outer ring pulses faintly on twos.

Escape from Birkenau was almost impossible. The camp had two rings of guards: an inner ring around the barracks, and an outer ring around the work sites. When a prisoner went missing, the outer ring stayed in place, day and night, for three days, while the ground between was searched. Then the SS assumed he had got away, and the guards were stood down. [F27]

So the plan was not to run. It was to hide inside the outer ring for three days, and then walk out.

> Picture: two faceless charcoal figures side by side, seen from behind.

His partner was Alfred Wetzler, a fellow Slovak, six years older, a prisoner since April 1942. [F28]

> Picture: the timber stack in a construction area: long boards, hard shadows, mud. The camera slowly circles. Tobacco flakes scattered in ink wash.

In a construction zone the prisoners called Mexico, there was a pile of timber, stacked for new barracks. With help from other prisoners, a hollow had been left inside it. [F29]

On the seventh of April 1944, the two men climbed in, and the boards were slid back over them. Around the pile, tobacco soaked in petrol had been scattered to confuse the dogs. [F30]

At evening roll call, they were missed. The sirens began. [F31]

> Picture: inside the hollow, almost black. Slivers of light between the boards. Searchlight sweeps cross the slits. Shadows of boots and a dog's legs pass, out of focus. Sound: distant sirens, boots, a dog, then silence.

For three days and three nights they lay in the dark, barely moving, as search parties and dogs passed close by. [F32]

On the evening of the tenth, they heard the order to stand the guards down. That night they pushed the boards aside, crawled out, and slipped away into the dark. [F33]

> Picture: open landscape in grey wash: fields, then forested hills. Two tiny figures cross the frame very slowly. Day and night are carried by the paper tone: cool grey for night, warm sepia for dusk.

They walked south, toward Slovakia, more than a hundred kilometres away, through fields and forest, mostly at night, with little food and no papers. For eleven days. [F34]

On the twenty-first of April, they crossed the border near the village of Skalité. [F35] A farmer put them in touch with helpers, and they were taken to the Jewish Council in Žilina. [F36]

## 6 · The report (6:30–8:40)

> Picture: a desk lamp's pool of light. Paper. A typewriter in silhouette. Pencil drawings of the camp layout appear as if drawn from memory, imperfect and corrected.

Žilina, the twenty-fifth of April. The two men began to dictate. They were questioned separately, and their accounts checked against each other and against the Council's records of deportations from Slovakia. [F37]

They described the camp's layout from memory, the gas chambers and crematoria, and the transports, country by country. [F38] The report was written in Slovak, then translated into German. [F39]

> Picture: a recreated period-style typewritten table (not a real document unless licensed) with the total circled in pencil, then a correction mark beside it.

It estimated that one million, seven hundred and sixty-five thousand Jews had been murdered at Birkenau in two years. [F40] That figure was too high. Historians today put the toll for Auschwitz's whole existence at about one point one million, nearly a million of them Jews. [F41] But the core of what the two men described has been confirmed many times over. [F42]

And the report carried a warning: Hungary's Jews were next. [F43]

> Picture: PHOTO. Hungarian Jews arriving at Birkenau, May–June 1944 (Auschwitz Album; a distant wide shot with no close faces). Slow push-in, heavy grain, sepia toning. Rights must be checked before use; see SOURCES.md.

Within days, copies were on their way to Jewish leaders in Budapest. [F44] In mid-May, the mass deportations from Hungary began. [F45] In about eight weeks, some four hundred and thirty-seven thousand people were deported, nearly all to Auschwitz. Most were murdered on arrival. [F46]

> Picture: the report's pages multiply and drift across the map: Žilina, Budapest, Geneva. Generic drawn newspaper columns bloom across Switzerland.

In June the report reached Switzerland, and its findings appeared in the Swiss press. [F47] Appeals and warnings reached Hungary's regent, Miklós Horthy: from the Pope, from the King of Sweden, from Washington. [F48]

On the seventh of July, Horthy ordered the deportations to stop. The last trains left two days later. [F49]

Historians still debate how much weight the report itself carried. But it was part of the pressure that stopped the trains before they reached Budapest, where some two hundred thousand Jews were still living. [F50] Many of them survived the war, though thousands more were murdered that winter, after Hungarian fascists seized power. [F51]

> Picture: PHOTO. Allied aerial reconnaissance photo of Auschwitz-Birkenau, 1944 (US National Archives). Slow pan across it. Rights must be checked; see SOURCES.md.

What the report could not do was stop what had already begun. It did not reach the families in the provincial ghettos. [F52] The Allies were asked to bomb the railway lines to Auschwitz. They refused. [F53] Allied aircraft had already photographed the camp, without anyone recognising what was in the pictures. [F54]

## 7 · After (8:40–10:00)

> Picture: the map lightens to a pale Atlantic and the outline of the Pacific coast. A small faceless figure at a laboratory bench, with glassware in fine pencil.

After the escape, Walter Rosenberg became Rudolf Vrba, and he kept that name for the rest of his life. [F55] That autumn he fought with the partisans in the Slovak National Uprising. [F56]

After the war he studied chemistry in Prague and earned a doctorate. [F57] In 1958, at a scientific conference in Israel, he defected. He lived in Israel, then Britain, and from 1967, Canada. [F58] For many years he taught pharmacology at the University of British Columbia, in Vancouver. [F59]

Alfred Wetzler stayed in Czechoslovakia. He wrote about Auschwitz under a pen name, and died in Bratislava in 1988. [F60]

Vrba kept testifying: in a memoir in 1963, in Claude Lanzmann's film Shoah, and in 1985, in a Toronto courtroom, as a witness against a Holocaust denier. [F61]

> Picture: rain on a window, ink running. The faceless figure, older now, seen from behind at a desk with the report.

And he never let go of one question: why the warning had not reached the people boarding the trains. He believed Hungary's Jewish leaders had kept it quiet while they negotiated with the SS, and that Rudolf Kasztner had paid for the lives of the nearly seventeen hundred people on his rescue train with silence. [F62] Many historians reject that judgement and argue that the leaders had few real choices. The argument has never been settled. [F63]

Rudolf Vrba died in Vancouver in March 2006, at the age of eighty-one. [F64]

> Picture: return to the opening railway line, now drawn going the other way, out of the frame. Fade to paper.

He escaped to deliver a warning. Part of it arrived in time. Part of it did not. He spent the rest of his life asking why. [F65]
